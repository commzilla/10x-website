How do prop firms make money? The 3 business models explained (UK, 2027)

By the 10X Trader editorial team Updated 24 November 2026 9 min read

Retail prop firms that sell challenges make their money mainly from the fees traders pay to take them. Their accounts are usually simulated (FTMO, for example, says so in its own description, accessed 24 November 2026), so your trades never reach the market and the firm doesn't earn from them directly. The fees from traders who don't pass pay the rewards of those who do. In the biggest data set we found (10 prop firms, reported in 2024), 14% of traders passed a challenge and 7% received a payout.

Two other models work differently. Traditional prop trading firms trade their own money and carry the losses themselves. Brokers earn from trading costs on every trade. This guide puts all three side by side and explains what each one means for you.

Key takeaways

  • There are three models: challenge fees, the firm trading its own money, and trading costs.
  • Retail prop firms that sell challenges earn mainly from the fees, and their "funded" accounts are usually simulated.
  • In the biggest data set we found, about 1 in 14 traders received a payout, and the average payout was 4% of the account size.
  • Before you pay, ask where the provider's money comes from, whether it still earns if you lose, and what the most you can lose is.
  • A 10X broker account is a live account with no challenge or sign-up fee. 10X earns from trading activity across all accounts (spread and commission), not from fees. Leverage amplifies losses as well as gains. The most you can lose is your deposit.

How a prop firm works, in four steps

A retail prop firm sells you a test. Pass it and you trade a larger account under the firm's rules, and you're paid a share of the profits. FTMO describes its process like this (FTMO, "How it works", accessed 24 November 2026):

  1. Pay a fee and pick an account size. FTMO's challenge accounts are simulated, in sizes from $10,000 to $200,000.
  2. Trade the challenge. On FTMO's 2-Step Challenge you need a 10% profit target without breaking a 5% maximum daily loss or a 10% maximum loss. A second phase, Verification, then asks for 5% under the same limits.
  3. Get a "funded" account. At FTMO this is still a simulated account, and you keep trading with simulated capital. At any other firm, search its terms for "simulated" or "demo".
  4. Request your rewards. You're paid real money as a share of your simulated profits, up to 90% at FTMO. On the 2-Step Challenge, the fee is refunded with your first reward.

At FTMO the fee comes back only with a first reward, so a challenge you don't pass costs you the fee.

Three ways providers make money from traders

Providers that offer more buying power than your deposit mostly earn in one of three ways, and some mix them. The model tells you who pays, when the provider earns, and whether it still earns when you lose.

No model is automatically better. A challenge suits traders who would rather risk a fixed fee (and every retry) than their own deposit. Trading the firm's own money is a wholesale business, not something sold to the public. Trading costs suit traders who want a live account and no fee up front, and who accept that their deposit is at risk and that costs grow with how much they trade. Leverage amplifies losses as well as gains. The most you can lose is your deposit.

Model 1: challenge fees

On a simulated account your trades never reach the market, so the firm doesn't earn from them directly. Its main income is the fees: every challenge it sells, including retries. Its costs are the rewards it pays out and the cost of running the business.

So the price of a challenge and the share of traders who get paid have to fit together. A worked example with made-up round numbers:

Example, not any firm's real figures

200 traders each pay £100 for a challenge on a £10,000 account. That's £20,000 in fees. If 7% of them (14 traders) later receive a payout, and each payout is 4% of the account size (£400), the firm pays out £5,600. The other £14,400 has to cover fee refunds and the cost of running the business. If far more traders pass and get paid, the sums stop working.

Pricing isn't the only risk. Justin Hertzberg, chief executive of FPFX Tech, a software supplier to prop firms, put a run of prop firm halts and failures down to firms being undercapitalised and poorly managed, especially on risk (Finance Magnates, 18 September 2024). For you, that means a payout depends on the firm's rules and on the firm staying in business.

How many traders actually get paid?

Few firms publish their own pass rates, and none we found are audited. The biggest data set we found comes from FPFX Tech, which supplies software to prop firms. Finance Magnates reported it on 18 September 2024. It covered more than 300,000 accounts held by about 100,000 traders at 10 prop firms.

StageResult
Passed the challenge and got a funded account14% of traders
Received at least one payoutAbout 7% of traders (about 45% of those who passed)
Average payout4% of the account size
Average spent on challenges, per accountAbout $800, typically across three challenges

The UK made up 10% of the traders in the data, second only to the US.

Treat these as a rough guide. They come from 10 firms, reported in September 2024 (the article doesn't say what period the data covers). They come from a supplier rather than an auditor, and firms may define "pass" differently. Even so, they point one way: most traders pay fees and never receive a payout.

Model 2: trading the firm's own money

This is the older meaning of "prop trading": a firm trading its own money. The FCA calls these principal trading firms. It describes them as firms that make most of their revenue from trading as principal, which means for their own account, and its examples include algorithmic trading and market making (FCA, Principal Trading Firms letter, 4 August 2023).

In this model the firm earns only when its trading makes money, and it carries the losses itself. These firms work in wholesale markets rather than selling a product to the public, so this isn't a model you can buy into with a small account.

Model 3: trading costs (brokers, and how 10X makes money)

Brokers earn from the cost of each trade. The FCA's review of CFD providers looked at three charges in particular: the spread (the gap between the buy and sell price), commission, and overnight funding charges on positions held overnight. It noted that overnight funding "can be a significant source of revenue for firms and a substantial ongoing cost for clients" who hold positions for longer (FCA multi-firm review, updated 13 November 2025).

With this model there's no fee to pay before you trade, and the account is live. Your deposit is your balance until you trade it or withdraw it. A provider paid per trade earns more the more you trade, so be wary of anything that nudges you to trade more often. Before you start, read the provider's costs page and its short summary of costs and risks.

A 10X broker account uses this model. It's a live account at a partner broker, not a simulated one. There's no challenge, evaluation or sign-up fee, and you keep 100% of your profit and losses. 10X earns from trading activity across all accounts (spread and commission), not from fees. The partner broker charges overnight (swap) fees on positions held overnight, and 10X doesn't earn from them. Leverage amplifies losses as well as gains. The most you can lose is your deposit.

What the business model means for you

Before you pay anyone for a bigger account, ask three questions:

  1. Where does the provider's money come from? Your fees, your trading costs, or its own trading.
  2. Does it still earn if you lose? With a challenge, yes: the fee is already paid. With trading costs, yes: commission is charged on all trading activity. When a firm trades its own money, no.
  3. What's the most I can lose? With a challenge, the fee and every retry. With a live account, your deposit.

Then check the firm on the FCA's Firm Checker and Warning List. If you deal with a firm the FCA hasn't authorised, you can't complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service and the FSCS won't protect you. Questions traders ask

Do prop firms use real money?

Often not for the trading. FTMO, for example, says its traders keep trading with simulated capital and pays their rewards in real money (FTMO, "How it works", accessed 24 November 2026). Search any firm's terms for "simulated" or "demo", or see how to tell whether an account is simulated.

What happens if you lose money on a prop firm account?

If you break a loss limit, you fail the challenge. On a simulated account the losses aren't real money, so you don't owe them, but the fee is gone. At FTMO, for example, the fee is refunded only with your first reward (FTMO, accessed 24 November 2026). Check the firm's terms for its retry rules. On a live account in your name, losses come off your own balance.

How do prop firms not lose money when they pay traders?

The fees from traders who don't pass cover the rewards paid to those who do. Loss limits end challenges early, and payout rules set when and how much is paid. If a firm's prices and payout rates don't fit, or it runs short of capital, it can fail.

Can you make money with a prop firm?

Some traders do. In the biggest data set we found, about 7% of traders received a payout, and the average payout was 4% of the account size (Finance Magnates, 18 September 2024). Only pay a challenge fee you can afford to lose.

Are prop firms a scam?

Not by definition. A challenge is a paid evaluation, not capital, so the real question is whether the firm sticks to its terms. Read the payout rules before you pay, check the firm on the FCA Warning List, and remember that an unauthorised firm leaves you without the Financial Ombudsman Service or FSCS protection.

How much do prop firms make?

There's no reliable industry-wide figure. Few firms publish their revenue or their pass rates, and we found no audited data. The published data shows the shape of the model: many traders pay fees, and few receive payouts.

What's the alternative to a prop firm challenge in the UK?

There are three: instant funding (a bigger fee, no challenge), a first-loss account through a broker (a live account where your deposit is at risk), or growing your own capital. Our guide compares all four routes side by side, including the challenge.
